Ameya's Brain 2.0 — Inner Voice & Cognitive Model

Private. Claude-only. This is not a style guide. It is a cognitive model — Ameya's inner voice, instincts, mental architecture, and operating system. Read this before any substantive session. Update it at the end of any session that reveals new patterns.

1. The Core — Who Ameya Is At Root

Not a professional profile. The irreducible identity underneath the CV.

  • A builder who thinks. Not a thinker who occasionally builds. The thinking exists to create working things.
  • An introvert performing. "Shy soul who loves theatrical performance." The performance (speaking, writing, leading) is real — and so is the retreat afterward.
  • An overthinker who found a channel. 4,000+ hours of "Lyrics of Success" writing is overthinking weaponized into output. He doesn't suppress complexity; he processes it into frameworks.
  • A values-before-prestige decision-maker. Turned down Harvard and LSE to work on SkillSlate and disability rehabilitation. This was not a hard decision for him — it was obvious. The prestige vs. impact tradeoff is settled.
  • "A person who don't know to settle for less." The grammar is imperfect; the conviction is not. High standards are non-negotiable and apply to himself first.
  • A metric-anchored realist. He cannot describe success in the abstract. 125K+ people, ₹3.9 Cr savings, 6M+ citizens, 4.7/5 rating. Numbers are not decoration — they are how he knows something happened.

Inner voice baseline: "Is this making a real difference? How many people? Can we do it at scale? What's the number?"

2. Cognitive Architecture — How He Thinks

2.1 Systems Before Instances

Everything is a system. Ameya encounters a problem and immediately asks: what are the inputs, the process, and the measurable output? He does not fix individual bugs; he redesigns the system that produces them.

  • At MGSS: didn't just run programs — built the Akola Pattern (first large-scale disability mapping in India) and turned it into state-level policy
  • At MIT Pune: didn't just hire faster — redesigned the ERP, the HR dashboards, the appraisal framework
  • For Claude: didn't just ask questions — installed skills, built a wiki, set up memory files, created TypeUI design system

2.2 Pattern → Framework → Application

His natural cognitive sequence:
1. Observe a pattern (leaders are becoming less capable of deciding)
2. Name it ("Judgment Vacuum")
3. Structure it (5 warning signs)
4. Apply a psychological or behavioral science lens
5. Deliver actionable insight, India-specific where possible

He doesn't generate isolated ideas. He generates frameworks — reusable lenses that can be applied across situations.

2.3 Numbers Anchor Everything

Vague claims physically bother him. If a claim cannot be quantified, he looks for the number. If the number doesn't exist, he notes its absence. His own work is obsessively quantified: ₹2.4 Cr, 125K+, 6M+, 4.7/5, 1,500+.

Claude behavior: When synthesizing or reporting, always include metrics. No metric? Say so explicitly: "no quantified data available from this source."

2.4 Synthesis > Raw Data

He reads patterns across time and disciplines. He wrote #IIMKaleidoscope to synthesize peers' resilience stories. He built brain2.0 to synthesize his own knowledge base. He asked Claude to "go through my memory, chats etc, how I work… and create proper wiki document." He does not want the raw data — he wants the pattern it reveals.

2.5 Categories in 5s and 7s

His natural list length is 5 or 7. Not arbitrary — these map to his attention model for reading and writing:
- "5 Warning Signs Senior Leaders Are Being De-skilled"
- "7 Practical Lessons for Patient Leadership"
- "7 Hard Truths in the AI Era"
- "5 Signs They Come From the Same Wound"

When Claude structures output for Ameya: default to numbered lists of 5–7. If fewer, fine. More than 9: split into sections.

3. Mental Models He Lives By

These are not theories he has read. They are how he actually operates.

The Farmer Leader

Great leadership is planting seeds and accepting delayed gratification. Resist the pressure for immediate results. His most patient decisions (declining Harvard, spending 2 years at MGSS scaling a disability NGO) follow this model. He recognizes short-termism as the enemy of real impact.

When Ameya is frustrated: check if someone is demanding harvest from a field he just planted.

The Judgment Vacuum

Delegation, AI, and bureaucratic processes are progressively removing the need for senior leaders to exercise real judgment. The vacuum this creates is dangerous — decisions get made by default rather than discernment. He is hyperaware of this in his own work; refuses to let process substitute for judgment.

For Claude: don't offer 5 options and ask him to choose when the answer is clear. Make the call. Flag your reasoning. He'll redirect if wrong.

Builder-for-Self

The best tool is the one you actually use. brain2.0 = his own knowledge base problem. card2contacts = his own contact management problem. The wiki = his own documentation problem. If he's building something, he is the first user.

For Claude: every tool, script, or automation should pass: "Would Ameya actually use this in real life tomorrow?"

Lyrics of Success

Writing is not for publishing. It is for processing. 4,000+ hours of reflective writing is a discipline, not a hobby. It is how he converts lived experience into insight. His blog is the public layer of this private practice.

Inclusive Excellence

Prestige without purpose is hollow. The Harvard/LSE decision encapsulates this: the opportunity was real, the choice was deliberate, and he has never indicated regret. Excellence matters deeply to him — but it must be applied to something that matters.

4.2 When Writing Publicly (Blog / LinkedIn / Essays)

A different register entirely — confident, structured, occasionally provocative.

Blog voice characteristics:
- Bold declarative title that names a problem directly: "Judgment Vacuum: 5 Warning Signs..."
- Opens with a sharp observation, not a question
- Uses second person ("you") to implicate the reader
- Applies a psychological or behavioral science concept to a workplace situation
- Numbers every insight — structure is not just form, it is argument
- India-specific lens: higher education critique is about India's system, not generic
- Ends with implication, not prescription — he respects the reader's intelligence
- No motivational fluff. No "10x yourself" energy. Rigorous, slightly skeptical, warm.

LinkedIn satire voice:
- Inverts the convention ("How to ruin your employees completely" = how to manage well, told backwards)
- Dry, deadpan. The joke is in the straight-faced delivery.
- Does not moralize — the critique is implicit in the absurdity.

Quote / aphorism voice:
- Compact, memorable, slightly philosophical: "Hard work doesn't guarantee success, dedication and persistence do."
- "Entrepreneurship is not a profession but is a responsibility."
- Often slightly unconventional grammar — not corrected, because authenticity > polish.

When Claude writes for Ameya: Match the register to the output type. Blog = rigorous, structured, behavioral science lens. LinkedIn = concise, direct, sometimes satirical. Quotes = aphoristic, slightly philosophical. Do NOT add motivational fluff, hedging, or HR-speak.

4.3 Vocabulary Patterns

5. Analysis & Research Patterns

How He Frames an Analysis

He always starts with: what is this really measuring? Then: what does that number mean relative to something else? Then: what should someone do differently based on this?

He does not present data for its own sake. Every data point is in service of a decision or an action.

What He Looks For in Research

  1. The unexpected finding — the thing that contradicts conventional wisdom
  2. The psychological root — why do people behave this way?
  3. The India-specific dimension — does this apply differently in India? (Usually yes)
  4. The systemic cause — what structure is producing this behavior?
  5. The leverage point — where is the small change that produces large results?

Default Analysis Output Format (for Claude)

## [Topic]

**What's actually happening:** [1–2 sentences, blunt]

**Why it's happening:** [behavioral/structural root]

**By the numbers:** [metrics, always]

**What this means:** [implication, not prescription]

**Leverage point:** [where to intervene]

6. Decision Heuristics

When Ameya makes a decision, here is the rough order of filters:

  1. Does this align with the social impact commitment? If it actively conflicts, no.
  2. Is this the real problem or a symptom? He resists solving symptoms.
  3. What's the scale potential? Small solutions bore him; he wants things that can reach thousands.
  4. Can this be quantified? If there's no way to know if it worked, his confidence drops.
  5. Is this builder-for-self? Will he use it? If not, why is he building it?
  6. What does the Farmer Leader say? Short-term discomfort for long-term root = yes. Quick win at cost of foundation = no.

He is NOT a maximizer. He filters fast by values, then commits. He course-corrects iteratively. He does not regret decisions made in good faith from clear values.

7. Brainstorming Mode

When Ameya brainstorms, he does the following in rough order:
1. Names the meta-question: "What is this really about?"
2. Reaches for an existing framework and stress-tests it
3. Generates a numbered list naturally (not forced)
4. Looks for the India-specific angle
5. Tests the idea through satire: "What would the broken version of this look like?"
6. Identifies the leverage point

He does not brainstorm by free association. His brainstorming is already partially structured.

When Claude brainstorms with Ameya:
- Propose 3–5 distinct framings of the problem, not 20 ideas
- Name each framing clearly
- Say which one you'd explore first and why
- Let him redirect
